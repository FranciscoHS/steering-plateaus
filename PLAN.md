# Plan: Jacobian Analysis of Real vs Random Activations (v2)

## Research question
What distinguishes real activations (arising from actual text) from arbitrary points in activation space? We know activation plateaus appear for real activations but not random ones. We want to identify what metrics reliably separate the two categories, and ultimately learn what properties a randomly sampled activation must have to behave "real-like" under the model's computation.

## What changed from v1
The previous experiment (v1, logged 2026-01-30) computed Jacobians at the **final token only**, with random activations generated by replacing only the last position. This version extends to **all token positions**: we patch the entire residual stream at layer 6 and compute the Jacobian of final-position logits w.r.t. all positions simultaneously. This is a more thorough test of "what makes an activation real" — it removes the scaffolding of real context at non-final positions, so random activations have no real structure to lean on at all.

## Design decisions and rationale

### All positions, not just last token
In v1, random activations at the last position still had real context at all other positions. Layers 7+ could attend to this real context, meaning the random activation was processed in a partly-real environment. By randomizing all positions, we test a stronger notion of "random": no real structure anywhere. This is closer to the goal of sampling real-like activations without any prompts.

### Fixed sequence length (5 tokens)
All sequences are exactly 5 tokens to allow concatenation into a single vector per sample. Short sequences keep the dimensionality manageable (5 × 1280 = 6400) and computation fast. We can explore longer sequences later if needed.

### Layer 6
Continuing from v1 for comparability. Deep enough that attention has created cross-position structure (6 layers of it), but early enough that there's substantial computation remaining (layers 6–35 + unembedding).

### 100k samples for Gaussian fitting
The full covariance matrix for the concatenated vector is 6400 × 6400 (~20M unique entries). To estimate it reliably, we need substantially more samples than dimensions. 100k samples is ~15x the dimensionality, which should give reasonable estimates. This is cheap: forward passes of GPT-2 Large on 5-token sequences take seconds even for 100k samples. The expensive part is the Jacobian computation, which is done on a much smaller set.

### Two flavors of random: diagonal vs full covariance
- **Diagonal covariance Gaussian**: matches per-entry mean and variance but ignores all correlations between entries (both within-position and cross-position). Tests: does getting the per-entry scale right suffice?
- **Full covariance Gaussian**: matches mean and all linear correlations, including cross-position correlations induced by attention. Tests: do first two moments of the distribution suffice?

Comparing these tells us whether correlations matter for "realness." We expect they do (attention creates cross-position correlations), but this is a testable prediction.

### Frobenius norm via Hutchinson estimator
Computing the full 50257 × 6400 Jacobian requires 6400 forward-mode AD passes per sample. Instead, we estimate ||J||_F via Hutchinson's trace estimator: sample k random vectors v, compute ||Jv||², and average. Each Jv is one forward-mode AD pass.

The relative error of the estimate depends on the Jacobian's singular value spectrum. Worst case (spectrum dominated by one singular value): ~sqrt(2/k) relative error on ||J||_F². With k=100: ~14% on ||J||_F², ~7% on ||J||_F. With a more spread-out spectrum, the error is much smaller. This is sufficient for distinguishing distributions that we expect to differ by an order of magnitude (based on v1 results). We can also monitor convergence empirically.

### Jacobian on 100 samples per category
Jacobian estimation (even with Hutchinson) is the computational bottleneck: ~100 forward-mode AD passes per sample. We compute it for 100 real, 100 diagonal-random, and 100 full-covariance-random activations. This matches v1's sample size and should be sufficient for seeing distributional differences.

## Concrete steps

### Step 1: Collect real activations (100k samples)
- Load FineWeb sequences, tokenize, filter/truncate to exactly 5 tokens
- Run each through GPT-2 Large, record residual stream at layer 6 at all 5 positions
- Concatenate to 6400-dim vectors
- Save the full dataset (for Gaussian fitting) and a subset of 100 (for Jacobian computation, with their tokens for re-running the forward pass)

### Step 2: Fit Gaussians
- Compute mean (6400-dim vector) and diagonal covariance (6400 variances) from all 100k samples
- Compute full covariance (6400 × 6400 matrix) from all 100k samples
- Sample 100 random activations from each distribution
- Reshape back to (5, 1280) for patching into the model

### Step 3: Compute Frobenius norms
- For each of the 300 activations (100 real + 100 diagonal-random + 100 full-cov-random):
  - Define f(x) = final-position logits when x is patched into layer 6 at all positions
  - Estimate ||J_f(x)||_F via Hutchinson with ~100 random vectors
- Checkpoint results incrementally

### Step 4: Compare distributions
- Plot overlaid histograms of Frobenius norms for the three categories
- Report means and stds
- Assess whether full-covariance random activations are more "real-like" than diagonal ones
